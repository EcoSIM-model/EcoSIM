1. What distinguishes EcoSIM in the crop-model literature?

EcoSIM’s strongest distinction is the breadth and detail of its coupled plant physiology, C–N–P biogeochemistry, roots, microbes, water, heat and transport. The defensible claim is a distinctive combination of mechanisms, rather than exclusive ownership of photosynthesis, crop rotations or greenhouse-gas simulation.

Comparison scope. The EcoSIM side is grounded in this checkout. The other-model side uses the primary papers and official documentation listed below, with versions identified where consequential. The comparison is an interpretation of model structures, not a common-data benchmark, runtime comparison or exhaustive survey of every extension.

Heritage matters. The repository describes EcoSIM as a library spun off from ecosys. Published ecosys work already documents hourly C/N/P coupling, root/mycorrhizal uptake and heat/water/solute transport. These are part of EcoSIM’s scientific lineage; the present review does not establish which mechanisms were newly introduced in EcoSIM, nor transfer ecosys validation results automatically to this revision. [60] [L13]

1.3 Claims supported by this comparison

Supported positioning: “EcoSIM provides a mechanistic platform for analyzing crop production together with C–N–P acquisition, physiological stress, microbial transformations and transport, enabling explicit investigation of production–environment tradeoffs.” This is a synthesis of the local implementation evidence, not a statement of superior predictive skill. [1] [2] [14] [19] [20] [21] [33] [34]

Shared capabilities: photosynthesis-based growth, multi-season crop management, rotations, nutrient stress and crop–soil feedbacks are not sufficient claims of uniqueness. Neither are GHG simulation, phosphorus modeling or an hourly subroutine in isolation. The qualifications above follow the cited papers and official documentation.

Cost of the added detail: chemical pools, microbial parameters, crop traits, soil hydraulics and boundary conditions introduce additional quantities to initialize and evaluate. They may support diagnosis and extrapolation when constrained by evidence, but also create uncertainty and parameter tradeoffs. No wall-clock speed, data-efficiency or out-of-sample accuracy advantage was measured here.

1.4 Match the model to the question

On structural scope, EcoSIM is a strong candidate when the question needs crop yield, nutrient fate, water stress and soil biogeochemistry evaluated together. AquaCrop offers a deliberately compact water-productivity formulation; DSSAT and APSIM provide extensive crop/farming-system structures; STICS has a documented intercrop formulation; WOFOST provides an established physiological production framework; ORYZA supplies a rice-specific comparator; and DNDC provides a direct biogeochemical/GHG comparator. These are model-selection inferences from the cited structures. For any proposed application, compare configured versions against the same observations before choosing on accuracy.

2. Overall assessment

EcoSIM has a substantial mechanistic foundation for crop simulation. It represents crop development, C3/C4 carbon assimilation, organ growth, root acquisition of water and nutrients, grain formation, and agricultural interventions within a coupled soil–plant–microbe system.

Its clearest application is investigating how crop production interacts with water, nitrogen, phosphorus, residues, soil carbon and gaseous losses. The expanded required-input guide specifies the data package, and the literature comparison explains which aspects of this process coupling distinguish EcoSIM from other crop models. A crop is represented through a plant functional type (PFT) and its traits, population, planting schedule and management; the crop name alone does not define all behavior. The active standalone driver calls plant growth alongside hydrology, microbial transformations, chemistry and transport. [1] [2] [3]

Practical judgment. The code supports detailed crop–environment experiments. Reliable prediction of yield, phenology or management benefits requires crop/site parameter checks and validation against observations. This review establishes implementation and example-input evidence; it does not establish prediction accuracy.
Production

Development, photosynthesis, allocation, seed number and grain filling.

Management

Planting, harvest, residue return, fertilizer, irrigation, tillage and drainage.

Environmental outcomes

Water and nutrient budgets, soil organic matter, fixation and trace-gas processes.

Evidence language: Implemented means a process was located in source and its integration was traced; Example means relevant input records exist and were read; Conditional means additional configuration, parameterization or targeted validation is needed. None of these labels means independently validated.

Climate suffixes change the effective crop

When the grid’s IETYPG is positive, the species reader combines the first four characters of the supplied crop name with that grid climate code. The dryland file’s “maize” and “soybean” therefore resolve to maiz31 and soyb31, because its grid uses 31. The rice management string says rice31, but the rice grid uses 34, making the effective entry rice34. When the grid code is zero, the supplied PFT code is retained. [5] [50] [54]

Check actual trait enums rather than inferring them from species names: ICTYP sets photosynthetic pathway, ISTYP annual/perennial habit, IDTYP determinate/indeterminate development, IPTYP photoperiod type and INTYP fixation type. For example, the selected soyb31 has IPTYP=2, which the current enum defines as long-day. This is an input value to inspect during crop calibration, not a botanical conclusion inferred from “soybean.” [4] [9] [45]

Coverage limit: the shared table contains spring wheat but no explicitly named winter-wheat family, and no named potato, cotton or sugarcane family. A belowground storage-organ option exists in the process code, but that alone does not establish a tested root/tuber crop parameterization. [45] [4] [14]

In prognostic mode, PlantModel transfers column and PFT state into the plant API, updates phenology, predicts uptake, grows active branches and roots, aggregates state, handles reseeding and transfers results back. GrowPlants invokes disturbance removal and dead-plant handling. CMake lists include these source modules, establishing that they belong to the built implementation. [2] [12] [3]

The crop examples use a 3600-second outer time step, with configured subcycling for water, heat, solute and gas calculations. This provides a route for subdaily stress and event responses, but temporal resolution should be reported with a result rather than described as universally fixed. [47] [51] [1]

Prognostic and prescribed vegetation answer different questions

With ldo_sp_mode, vegetation structure is prescribed/interpolated and the plant driver takes an uptake-oriented path instead of the prognostic phenology and growth sequence. This can support experiments with externally supplied vegetation, but a run with prescribed LAI/root structure should not be presented as an independent prediction of crop development or yield. 6. Establishment, phenology and stress

Planting inputs provide a date, initial population and planting depth, with an optional maximum population. The reader distinguishes inactive, living and harvested plants. Emergence depends on implemented plant growth and seeding depth; it is not just the planting date copied into the emergence field. The phenology code also represents branching/tillering and node development. [6] [10]

0 Planting → 1 Emergence → 2 Floral initiation → 3 Jointing → 4 Elongation → 5 Heading → 6 Anthesis → 7 Begin seed fill → 8 Seed number set → 9 Seed mass set → 10 End seed fill

Development responds to temperature, water status, oxygen stress and photoperiod, with annual/perennial and determinate/indeterminate branches. The environmental factor routine applies water-potential and oxygen effects to annual development; floral initiation uses node state, daylength and configured critical photoperiod/sensitivity. Parameters controlling favorable-condition and senescence hours support leaf-on/off behavior. [10] [11] [9] [4]

Temperature and water stress affect multiple pathways: development, leaf expansion, stomata, respiration, allocation and reproduction. Grain formation includes high-temperature and chilling effects on seed number. Root oxygen supply connects wet-soil conditions to plant function. Consequently, changing weather or irrigation can alter both biomass accumulation and its timing. [12] [14] [21]

Winter crop qualification. Cold-dependent leaf phenology exists, but this review did not establish a cultivar-calibrated winter-cereal vernalization and frost-hardiness package. The names VRNLI/VRNXI are described by the current trait reader as favorable-condition hours for leafout and senescence-condition hours for leafoff; those names alone do not prove a winter-wheat vernalization model. [9] [10]

7. Photosynthesis, growth and yield formation

Carbon acquisition and organ growth

Separate C3 and C4 routines represent carbon fixation with light, CO₂, temperature, water and nutrient feedbacks. Canopy radiation and vertical leaf allocation connect foliage structure to light interception. Growth then allocates resources among leaves, petioles/sheaths, stalks, reserves, reproductive structures and roots, while charging maintenance and growth respiration and allowing remobilization during senescence. [15] [16] [13] [12]

This structure permits experiments on CO₂ and climate responses, planting density, resource limitation and source–sink feedbacks. Those responses remain predictions of the configured physiology; they are not guaranteed fertilizer-response or yield-response curves for an arbitrary cultivar.

Grain yield emerges from source and sink development

GrainFillOnBranch builds potential seed sites from stalk growth before anthesis, constrains realized seed set using nonstructural C, N and P, modifies seed number under thermal stress, develops potential individual seed size, and transfers reserves into grain. Filling depends on temperature, remaining sink capacity, available reserves and grain/reserve stoichiometry. Failure to fill for sufficient hours contributes to physiological maturity. [14]

Remaining grain sink C = max(0, potential C per seed × seed number − current grain C)

The source explicitly limits the hourly transfer by this remaining sink before C/N/P constraints. A belowground-organ flag selects root-zone rather than canopy temperature in the filling-rate branch. These are mechanistic options; their existence does not imply that every harvestable storage organ has a complete, validated crop formulation. [14] [4]

Convert model yield to the quantity actually measured

GRAIN_C_pft is standing grain carbon per ground area, not total harvested dry matter. For carbon fraction fC in grain dry matter, a compatible carbon amount Cgrain in g C m⁻² converts as follows:

Dry grain yield [kg ha⁻¹] = 10 × Cgrain / fC
Yield at wet-basis moisture fraction m = dry grain yield / (1 − m)

The factor 10 is the area/mass conversion from g m⁻² to kg ha⁻¹. Supply and document crop-appropriate carbon and moisture fractions. Use the amount exported at harvest for harvested yield; a preharvest standing pool can differ because of harvest efficiency, retained material and other losses. Grain N and P allow stoichiometric analyses, but protein, oil and commercial grade require additional conversion or modeling assumptions. [36] [37] [22]

8. Roots, nutrients and legumes

EcoSIM represents root growth, branching, geometry and resource allocation through soil layers, with root and mycorrhizal pathways. Water uptake and root–shoot transport couple soil water availability to canopy water potential. Nutrient acquisition includes NH₄, NO₃ and phosphate uptake with root/mycorrhizal uptake parameters, available substrate and oxygen constraints. [18] [17] [19]

For crop studies, this provides mechanisms by which rooting depth, root surface, soil layering, fertilizer placement and competition can change nutrient capture and drought response. Root/mycorrhizal trait choices and hydraulic parameters therefore deserve validation alongside aboveground growth. A soil profile that is too shallow or hydraulically inappropriate can constrain a crop even when canopy traits are well calibrated.

Legume capability is supported by root-nodule biochemistry and the supplied fixing crop entries, including soybean, alfalfa and clover. Fixation is coupled to nodule substrate, respiration and nutrient/oxygen conditions, allowing the carbon cost of N acquisition and its interaction with soil mineral N to be represented. Fixation output should be evaluated directly rather than inferred from a crop label. [20] [45] [36]

Plant structural and nonstructural element accounting centers on C, N and P. Soil chemistry includes other ions, and salt-related osmotic stress reaches the plant interface, but this review did not establish a separate crop K-deficiency growth response comparable to the N/P pathways. Likewise, osmotic stress support does not by itself establish species-specific Na/Cl toxicity or ion exclusion. [4] [19] [35] [34]

NH₃ fertilizer delivery: confirmed defect and correction

The base revision accepted NH3Band and broadcast NH₃, accumulated their fertilizer reservoirs and released NH₃ through soil chemistry. The fast transport routine then omitted the staged positive NH₃ source: fertilizer N could leave its reservoir without entering the soil gas pool. The correction adds that source to gas-phase NH₃ before sink limiting and includes source minus sink in both fast-transport and soil-production budgets. Gas–water exchange and chemistry can subsequently redistribute the delivered N. This repairs an existing transfer; it does not introduce additional fertilizer input. [26] [34] [63] [64]

A focused regression checks delivered mass, phase/layer routing, both budgets and sink carryover using the production procedures. It passes with the fix and fails against the base revision. Separate one-day broadcast and banded application runs also completed with finite outputs and recorded the prescribed 2 g N/m² amendment. [65]

Harvest flags and records

Canonical action codes are 0 none, 1 grain, 2 all aboveground, 3 pruning, 4 grazing, 5 fire and 6 herbivory. Termination codes are independently 0 no action, 1 terminate and 2 terminate/reseed. Cutting proxy and thinning meanings depend on the action. [4] [7] [22]

DDMMYYYY, ICUT, JCUT, HCUT, PCUT,
ECUT11, ECUT12, ECUT13, ECUT14,
ECUT21, ECUT22, ECUT23, ECUT24

The first ECUT group controls removal from the PFT’s tissue classes; the second controls ecosystem export of removed material. The production/soil-carbon consequence depends on both. The reader stores plant events in daily arrays; multiple records for the same slot/day overwrite those array entries rather than defining a general ordered sequence of intraday operations. Many non-grazing harvest operations and fertilizer application occur at the model’s solar-noon hour. [7] [22] [26]

10. Irrigation, drainage and rice

Scheduled and automatic irrigation

Scheduled irrigation specifies a date, amount, start/end hours, application depth, pH and nutrient/ion chemistry. The reader validates the hour window and nonnegative amount. Same-day events can add water if chemistry and depth agree; incompatible same-day properties are rejected. Automatic irrigation operates within a selected season/hour window and computes water needed to reach a soil-water target. [27] [28]

The automatic decision uses either soil-water depletion or canopy water potential. The latter explicitly reads PSICanPDailyMin_pft(1,...): the first PFT controls the decision, an important constraint in mixtures. The refill target in the code is:

θtarget = min(porosity, θWP + CIRRA × (θFC − θWP))

This uses a fraction of the interval between wilting point and field capacity. Describing CIRRA merely as “fraction of field capacity” loses that distinction. The requested volume is calculated over a depletion zone and distributed into scheduled hourly depths, which the weather pathway converts using cell area. [28] [29]

Application depth and soil transport provide a basis for surface/subsurface watering scenarios. This review did not establish dedicated emitter geometry, pressure networks, conveyance losses, water allocation or pumping-energy models. Those would require explicit external assumptions or extensions.

What the supplied rice example demonstrates

The rice case specifies annual planting and grain harvest during 2008–2016. Its soil management has fertilizer records, tillage records and daily type-23 prescribed water-table values. Every selected annual irrigf value is NO. Thus its wet-soil forcing is established through the water-table management path, not the explicit irrigation-event path. [51] [52] [53] [31]

Rice-relevant mechanisms include root porosity/gas exchange, oxygen limitation, soil saturation, anaerobic decomposition, methanogenesis, methane oxidation and gas transport. These make EcoSIM capable of examining coupled crop–redox–methane responses, subject to validated boundary conditions and process parameters. [21] [30] [32] [33] [1]

For an alternate wetting/drying experiment, define the water-level or irrigation control explicitly and check the resulting soil saturation, root oxygen, crop development, grain formation, CH₄ and N₂O. A prescribed water-table trajectory alone does not quantify irrigation demand or demonstrate an autonomous paddy management controller.

11. Soil carbon, nutrient losses and greenhouse gases

Crop removal and residue return feed a soil system with microbial functional groups, organic substrate turnover, nutrient transformations, solution chemistry and transport. The compiled microbial library includes nitrifiers, denitrifiers, fermenters, methanogens and methanotrophs, alongside aerobic organisms. This gives the model a process basis for N transformations and aerobic/anaerobic carbon cycling. [32] [33] [34] [1]

That coupling is valuable for questions such as how residue retention changes soil carbon and mineral N, how fertilizer timing interacts with wet soil and gaseous N loss, or how drainage changes rice CH₄ while affecting crop water supply. NH₃, nitrogen oxides and methane require checking the relevant chemistry, gas and transport settings, as well as the reported flux definitions. A process being compiled is not evidence that it is enabled and well parameterized in every case. [1] [26] [21] [38]

Management comparisons must include their full system boundary: fertilizer/manure and seed inputs, harvested exports, retained residues, dissolved losses and gas exchange. A larger standing crop or lower seasonal NEE does not by itself demonstrate net ecosystem carbon storage. EcoSIM has plant element-balance checks and ecosystem diagnostics, but numerical balance and agreement with observations are separate tests. [43] [22] [38] [10]

Long-term SOC or emission-factor claims therefore need spin-up assessment, realistic management history, boundary-flux checks and multi-year observations. For climate metrics, distinguish g C as CH₄ from g CH₄, and g N as N₂O from g N₂O before applying any externally chosen conversion factors.

12. Rotations, cover crops, forage and mixed stands

Annual rotations and fallow are explicit capabilities

The dryland example alternates maize and soybean in one active PFT slot across six annual records. Soil pools persist as crop identity and management change, supporting carryover effects of previous crops. Fallow is represented with blank trailing PFT slots that retain residue identity while no longer growing. The reader requires plants to be dead before a slot becomes fallow and rejects woody/nonwoody slot changes with standing dead material when litter interpretation would become inconsistent. [48] [5]

Mixtures and forage are configurable, with added evaluation needs

Multiple PFTs can coexist in a column, sharing radiation and soil resources. The trait table includes alfalfa and clovers, and the disturbance system supports cutting/grazing without necessarily terminating the stand. These provide ingredients for forage, cover-crop mixtures and intercrop experiments. Species competition, regrowth after cuts, population dynamics and management scheduling still require targeted validation; an input file’s multiple slots do not establish agronomic performance. [45] [16] [2] [22] [23]

The standard planting reader provides one planting-information string per PFT slot/year, while harvest allows multiple events. Year-specific species selection is not a general within-year species-replacement engine. Double cropping, relay planting, transplanting and perennial ratooning should therefore be treated as specific configurations to demonstrate, including initialization and residue accounting, rather than assumed from annual rotation support. [6] [7] [5]

14.4 Crop traits and management information

Crop traits cover C3/C4 biochemistry (ICTYP, VCMX, VCMX4), life cycle/development/photoperiod (ISTYP, IDTYP, IPTYP, GROUPX, XDL, XPPD), morphology/optics, root geometry and hydraulics, nutrient-uptake kinetics, fixation/mycorrhizae, organ C:N:P and construction costs, and seed-site/size/filling traits (STMX, SDMX, GRMX, GFILL). Most are reusable model parameters, not quantities that must all be newly measured at every site. Their provenance and applicability still need justification. [9] [45]

Site-specific crop management must supply crop sequence, planting dates, plants m⁻² and depth, termination/harvest dates and efficiencies, and the intended residue treatment. Soil management adds nutrient form, elemental dose, placement/depth, organic C/N/P additions, tillage, water application and drainage controls. Fertilizer input amounts use the reader’s g m⁻² basis; for elemental N or P doses, 1 g m⁻² = 10 kg ha⁻¹. Do not substitute commercial fertilizer-product mass for its elemental nutrient dose. Amendment minerals and ion concentrations require their own declared conventions. [6] [7] [25] [26]

14.5 Atmospheric gases, initial conditions and reproducibility

The atmospheric dataset uses a monthly time dimension and year timestamps encoded as calendar year + month/12. January is Y + 1/12 and December is Y + 1. CO2 is ppmv, while CH4 and N2O are ppbv in the selected historical dataset and are converted as needed internally. Full-year coverage is checked for gases not fixed by overrides. Maintain a readable file path even when all three gases are fixed, because the reader opens the dataset first. [57]

For a cold start, initial soil stocks, water and chemistry must represent the start date or be brought to an appropriate state through a documented spin-up. For a restart, preserve compatible model state and schedules; a checkpoint does not remove the need for remaining weather, parameter and management inputs. Yield, LAI, phenology, ET, soil moisture, mineral nutrients and gas-flux observations are evaluation data, not mandatory files consumed automatically by the crop driver. [42] [1] [58]

Fertilizer amount: AMENDED_N_col

AMENDED_N_col reports cumulative N amendment for the current year in g N/m²; multiply by 10 for kg N/ha. It includes all mineral fertilizer forms, including NH3Band and NO3Band, and organic residue/manure N routed through the amendment pathway. It aggregates forms and is already selected by both dryland namelists. The corrected metadata describes total fertilizer N/P amendment, removing the misleading restriction to “organic.” [26] [38] [69]

The accumulator resets in annual initialization. History uses averaging flag A: daily means on application days can lie between the pre-event and post-event totals. Use a post-event value or adequately resolved differences, respecting year resets; summing cumulative samples over time would overcount. [39] [38]

Confirmed harvest-output qualification. The three PFT HVST_*_FLX_pft fields are registered with per-hour units and averaging flag A, but HistUpdatePlant assigns annual cumulative harvested C/N/P divided by cell area. The disturbance code increments those totals, the API preserves them, and annual setup resets them; plant initialization can also reset them. The column ECO_HVST_* fields use the corresponding column cumulative totals, with mass-per-area units and averaging. Summing these history samples as hourly harvest fluxes would overcount. [36] [37] [22] [40] [39] [41] [38]

Extract harvest export from adequately resolved changes in the cumulative state, respecting annual and crop-initialization resets and history averaging. A difference between daily means around an abrupt event is not necessarily the event amount. For the cleanest event audit, use hourly history/checkpoints and compare before/after state. The harvest accumulator includes the exported mixture of affected tissues; it is not inherently a grain-only yield variable.

Area and unit checks: the history update divides grain and other plant totals by ground-cell area. Some internal comments use d-2 notation; that should not be interpreted automatically as “per day squared.” Trace allocation and history conversion when working with internal arrays. Standing grain, whole-plant harvest C and dry grain mass are different quantities. [37] [14] [22]

Sign checks: QVegTransp_pft is described as negative into the atmosphere, while cumulative transpiration uses a positive convention. Surface CH₄ flux metadata also uses negative into the atmosphere and excludes specified wet-deposition pathways. Normalize signs and included pathways before comparing treatment totals. [36] [38]

16. Limits and priorities for credible crop applications

BoundaryWhat is establishedWhat remains to demonstrate
Cultivar transferTraits control crop development, photosynthesis, roots and grain.An evaluated trait set for each cultivar/environment; realistic interactions among those traits.

Long-term outcomesPersistent soil/plant state, budgets and restart machinery exist.Adequate spin-up, validated soil carbon trends, complete gas/loss accounting and crop-transition restart tests.

“Not established” describes the scope of this source review, not proof that no related code or external workflow exists. Generic plant or disturbance options should be promoted to crop-specific capabilities only after a concrete configuration and evaluation demonstrate the desired behavior.

Validation sequence

  1. Input and accounting audit: effective traits, chronology, units, seeded inputs, harvested exports, residue routing, water inflows/outflows and C/N/P balance.
  2. Seasonal crop trajectory: emergence, leaf area, biomass, anthesis and maturity, rooting, grain number/size and yield on the same moisture basis as observations.
  3. Resource response: fertilizer-rate/timing/placement, drought/irrigation, wet soil, fixation and N/P uptake; verify mechanisms as well as final yield.
  4. Rotation or perennial persistence: crop replacement, fallow, residue carryover, repeated cuts and year-boundary behavior.
  5. Environmental performance: ET, soil water, drainage, mineral nutrients, dissolved losses, CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O with consistent signs and units.
  6. Numerical reproducibility: focused smoke runs and uninterrupted-versus-restart comparisons around management and annual transitions, followed by uncertainty/sensitivity analysis.

Restart code stores extensive crop state, including planting/harvest dates, crop identity and harvested-element totals; annual restart tests are present. These resources support validation, but their existence is not a passing result. A successful build or finite-valued short run does not validate crop science. [42] [56] [43]

Application judgment

Strong implementation basis: crop–water–nutrient interactions, annual maize/soybean rotation experiments, residue/fertilizer interventions and managed wet-soil crop biogeochemistry. Additional demonstration needed: reliable cultivar-specific forecasts, complex multiple-crop calendars, specialized storage-organ crops and engineered water-management demand. The highest-value next step is a full-season crop benchmark with validated management accounting and event-resolved yield extraction.
